> Agree the rules with everyone in the firm before anyone uses a prompt. Then fill in each prompt's blanks. The more context you give, the better the answer.
>
> AI drafts and you decide. Check every figure, rule and reference it gives you against the source before it reaches a client. The tools change quickly, so review the pack and the rules every quarter.
>
> Which tools may hold your clients' data, and on what terms, is a legal and professional question, not a technical one. Have your rules checked by an adviser who knows data protection and your professional body's requirements.

## Our rules for AI and client data

[Your firm's name]. Agreed on [Date]. Next review on [Date].

1. We use only these tools for client work: [Each tool, and the plan or account type we use it on]
2. We never put any of this into an AI tool: [Identity numbers, bank details, passwords, anything a client has marked confidential, and anything your adviser adds]
3. Before pasting a document, we replace names with roles, such as "the client", "the director" or "Supplier A"
4. We tell clients that we use AI tools, and how: [Where, such as the engagement letter or privacy notice, in wording your adviser has checked]
5. Nothing an AI tool writes goes to a client until a person has checked it and is willing to sign it
6. When a tool gets something wrong in a way that matters, we record it here: [Where the record is kept]

## The prompts

### 1. Reply to a client's email

> You are an accountant writing to a small business owner who is not an accountant. Their email: [Paste it, with names removed]. The facts of their situation: [Facts]. Draft a reply of no more than 150 words, in plain British English, that answers the question, says what you need from them and gives one next step. Do not state any tax rule, rate or date. Where one is needed, write CHECK HERE in capitals.

### 2. Explain a month's figures in plain words

> Here are a client's figures for [Month], with the same month last year: [Paste the figures, with no names]. Write five sentences an owner would understand: one on sales, one on margin, one on costs, one on cash and one on what to watch next month. Use only the figures given. Where something needs a reason the figures do not show, write a question for me instead of guessing.

### 3. Turn rough notes into a checklist

> Here is how we do [The task, such as month-end for a retail client], as rough notes: [Paste the notes]. Turn them into a numbered checklist a new member of staff could follow. Keep my order unless a step clearly depends on a later one, and tell me when you move one. List any step that seems to be missing.

### 4. Prepare for a first call

> A prospective client runs [Type of business] with about [Number] staff. They told me: [What they said, with no names]. List ten questions an accountant should ask on a first call to understand the business, its records and what the owner wants. Group them under those three headings.

### 5. Plan a piece of writing

> I write for [The clients your positioning sentence describes]. This month's idea is: [Idea]. Give me an outline for a 600-word article, with a title, the question it answers, three sections and a closing action. Then write a LinkedIn post of about 200 words that makes the same point.

### 6. Summarise a long document

> Summarise the document below for an accountant who must act on it. Give the five points that matter most, every date and figure it states, and any question it leaves open. Quote the document's own words for anything that sets a duty or a limit. The document: [Paste it, with client details removed]

### 7. Check a draft before it goes

> Here is a draft [Email, letter or report] to a client: [Paste the draft]. Point out anything unclear, anything that could be read two ways and anything that reads as a promise I may not be able to keep. Do not rewrite it. List each problem with a suggested fix.

## Before anything AI helped with goes out

- [ ] Nothing left in that the rules above keep out
